# Architecture family comparison: classical vs. embedding vs. hybrid ER

Research spike for issue #8 (ML Challenge 2026 entity-resolution hackathon). Scope: gather grounded facts on three candidate architecture families for matching Source 2/Source 3 business records against the ~2.2M-entity Source 1 reference set. No recommendation is made here — synthesis happens in a separate document.

Task shape assumed throughout: ~2.2M reference entities (Source 1), ~5M (Source 2) + ~5.3M (Source 3) records to match, US/India training data with France unseen at test time, macro F_0.5 scoring, no external API calls, MIT/Apache-2.0 model ≤8B params, AWS on-demand compute, 2-day budget.

---

## (A) Classical: blocking + hand-engineered similarity + GBT

### Architecture landscape

Blocking-based record linkage is the long-established default for large-scale ER and is the design used by every mainstream open-source toolkit:

- **Splink** (UK Ministry of Justice) blocks via explicit SQL-style rules (`block_on(...)`) run on DuckDB/Spark, and documents that "blocking rules are the most important determinant of the performance of your linkage job" — the workflow is to start with strict rules and loosen them, since OR-based rules degrade to near-full cross joins in most SQL engines. [Splink: What are Blocking Rules?](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink3_legacy_docs/topic_guides/blocking/blocking_rules.html) · [Blocking Rule Performance](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/topic_guides/blocking/performance.html)
- A published Splink benchmark deduplicated **7 million** synthetic-person records using 9 blocking rules (name/DOB/geo composites), generating ~1 billion candidate comparisons, completing in **~2 minutes** on a high-spec EC2 instance and **~25 minutes** on an 8 vCPU/16 GB "laptop-class" instance (c6g.2xlarge), for under $1 of compute. [Deduplicating 7 million records in two minutes with Splink](https://www.robinlinacre.com/fast_deduplication/)
- **py_entitymatching / Magellan** (University of Wisconsin) is the reference academic EM toolkit: it offers four blocker types (attribute-equivalence, overlap, rule-based, black-box) chained into a blocking sequence, then computes similarity-feature vectors (edit distance, Jaccard on q-grams, etc.) per candidate pair and trains classifiers including Random Forest, evaluated by F1. [Magellan project docs / how-to guide](https://pradap-www.cs.wisc.edu/magellan/how-to-guide/how_to_guide_magellan.pdf) · [py_entitymatching guides](http://magellan-example-test.org/packages/py-entitymatching/docs/user_manual/guides.html)
- **Zingg** (AGPL-3.0 licensed, so not usable as-is for a license-constrained deliverable, but architecturally representative) uses an auto-learned blocking model plus a trained similarity classifier, and states blocking cuts record comparisons "by up to 90%" at million-record scale. [Zingg + Neo4j docs](https://www.zingg.ai/documentation-article/entity-resolution-and-analysis-with-zingg-and-neo4j) · [zinggAI/zingg GitHub](https://github.com/zinggAI/zingg)
- **Dedupe.io** (MIT-licensed) generates blocking predicates via active learning (tens of predicate functions combined into compound rules), then a logistic-regression-style classifier over similarity features, followed by hierarchical clustering; documented to scale "from a few thousand records to several million." [dedupeio/dedupe GitHub](https://github.com/dedupeio/dedupe)
- A directly analogous production system, **"Fast Record Linkage for Company Entities"**, blocks a reference database of up to **150 million** company records using **MinHash/LSH** (6-row/30-band config chosen as the best memory/recall tradeoff), reducing candidates to **~23,600 per query** against the 150M table, then scores candidates with a hand-built rule tree (90% Jaccard + 10% Levenshtein on company name, plus tokenized-Levenshtein address matching, postal code, Haversine city distance, SIC-code industry matching). Reported recall/precision: **91%/65%** on a 450-company Swiss ground-truth set and **89%/89%** on a 55K-record accounting dataset, versus baselines of 57% recall (trivial string match) and 72% recall (Apache Solr). [arXiv:1907.08667](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.08667)
- Gradient-boosted trees are the standard classifier on top of these similarity features: **XGBoost** (Apache-2.0) and **LightGBM** (MIT) are both permissively licensed and CPU-friendly. [dmlc/xgboost LICENSE](https://github.com/dmlc/xgboost/blob/master/LICENSE) · [lightgbm-org/LightGBM LICENSE](https://github.com/lightgbm-org/LightGBM/blob/v2.0.12/LICENSE)

### Blocking recall ceiling / reduction ratio at scale

Standard evaluation metrics in the blocking literature: **Pairs Completeness (PC)** = recall of true-match pairs retained by the candidate set; **Reduction Ratio (RR)** = 1 − (comparisons after blocking / all-pairs comparisons). [Papadakis et al., "Blocking and Filtering Techniques for Entity Resolution: A Survey," ACM Computing Surveys](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06167)

- **Token blocking**: the survey characterizes it as yielding "very high PC, at the cost of very low PQ [pair quality/precision] and RR" — i.e. token blocking is recall-safe but leaves a large candidate set unless paired with a cleaning/meta-blocking step. No cross-dataset percentage table was extractable from this particular survey PDF, so treat this as a qualitative, not quantitative, claim. [Papadakis survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06167)
- **Sorted neighborhood**: recall/precision is governed by the sliding-window size `w` — "a small w leads to high PQ and RR but low PC and vice versa for a large w." The survey separately notes sorted neighborhood "consistently underperforms other methods because it is incompatible with block/comparison-cleaning techniques." [Papadakis survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06167)
- **Phonetic encoding (Soundex/Metaphone)**: not empirically quantified in the surveys checked; Splink documents phonetic keys (Soundex, Metaphone, Double Metaphone) as usable blocking-key transforms but does not publish reduction/recall numbers for them. [Splink blocking rules](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink3_legacy_docs/topic_guides/blocking/blocking_rules.html)
- **Geographic/postal blocking + MinHash-on-name** at real scale: the 150M-record company-linkage system above reduced ~150M candidates to ~23,600 per query (reduction ratio on the order of 99.98%) while holding 89–91% recall on its two labeled test sets — the closest found real-world analog to this challenge's scale and domain (noisy company names + address). [arXiv:1907.08667](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.08667)
- **Critical failure mode for name-token blocking**: a 2026 benchmark on corporate-family resolution (matching company names that may share *no* surface tokens, e.g. subsidiary vs. parent brand names) found that **no existing method recovers more than 3% of "invisible" pairs individually (6.8% in union)**, and that **93.2% of these true links never even enter the candidate set** — i.e., under token-overlap-style blocking, recall is capped by candidate generation, not by the downstream classifier. This is a cautionary data point for how DBA/trade-name and heavily-abbreviated pairs in this challenge's Source 2/3 could sit below any token-blocking recall ceiling. [Corporate-Family Resolution Is Not a String-Matching Problem (arXiv:2609.04269)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04269)
- A controlled academic comparison ("blocking vs. nearest-neighbor search") found that on synthetic Dirty-ER datasets scaled from 10K to 2M entities, **both traditional blocking workflows and embedding-based kNN search were tuned to reach PC ≥ 0.9**, i.e. recall ceilings of ~90%+ are achievable by classical blocking at this scale when workflows are properly tuned — the paper's differentiator was runtime/scalability, not achievable recall. [How to reduce the search space of ER: Blocking or NN search? (arXiv:2202.12521)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12521)

### Deep-ER comparison point (for context, not this family)

Ditto (BERT-family fine-tuned matcher, not embeddings-as-feature) reports a **9.43% average F1 gain** (up to 32%) over prior non-neural SOTA across 13 standard EM benchmark datasets, and **96.5% F1** on a real two-company-dataset matching task (789K × 412K records) — useful as an upper anchor for what a fully neural matcher can reach on business-entity data, though Ditto itself is a cross-encoder/classifier over pretrained LM, not this project's classical family. [Li et al., "Deep Entity Matching with Pre-trained Language Models," VLDB 2020 (arXiv:2004.00584)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00584)

### Language/script coverage relevant to Hindi transliteration in Source 2

- multilingual-E5 is initialized from **XLM-RoBERTa**, trained on the CC-100 corpus covering 100 languages; XLM-R's documentation explicitly notes it includes **"romanized variants of some non-Latin languages such as Bengali, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu"** — i.e., XLM-R-derived models (multilingual-E5) have at least some exposure to Latin-script ("Hinglish"-style) Hindi during pretraining, in addition to native Devanagari. [Conneau et al., "Unsupervised Cross-lingual Representation Learning at Scale" (arXiv:1911.02116)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116) · [XLM-RoBERTa model docs](https://huggingface.co/FacebookAI/xlm-roberta-base)
- LaBSE lists Hindi among its 109 supported languages and was explicitly trained for cross-script/cross-language bitext alignment, which is the closest training objective to "match a Devanagari business name to its Latin-script equivalent." [LaBSE model card](https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/LaBSE)
- **None of the checked model cards report an evaluation specific to transliterated business/entity-name matching.** Their reported benchmarks are sentence-level: Mr. TyDi and MTEB for E5, Tatoeba bitext retrieval for LaBSE, STS/paraphrase mining for mpnet — all are full-sentence semantic-similarity or passage-retrieval tasks, not short noisy proper-noun matching. This is a real evaluation gap for this challenge's use case and should be treated as unverified, not assumed.
- Dedicated Hindi-transliteration resources exist and could be used to build a quick validation set (not for training a matcher against, but to sanity check an embedding model's transliteration robustness): **AI4Bharat's Aksharantar** dataset (26M Indic-language ↔ English transliteration pairs across 21 languages) and the **Dakshina** dataset are the standard references. [IndicNLP catalog](https://github.com/AI4Bharat/indicnlp_catalog) · [IndoNLP 2025 shared task on romanized Indo-Aryan transliteration (arXiv:2501.05816)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05816)

### Embedding-based blocking effectiveness vs. classical, at scale

- A controlled experimental analysis of 12 embedding families for ER blocking (Word2Vec/GloVe/FastText, BERT-family, SentenceBERT-family) across 17 datasets (up to 2M entities, synthetic Dirty-ER) found: **SentenceBERT-style models (S-GTR-T5) sustained 0.800 recall at k=10 neighbors at 2M entities**, versus **FastText degrading to 0.415** (a 54% relative drop) at the same scale; SentenceBERT-style embeddings also beat **DeepBlocker** (a dedicated learned-blocking baseline) by ~15% recall on most datasets, though DeepBlocker was faster at small k. Plain (non-fine-tuned) BERT embeddings underperformed due to "poor discriminativeness" without task-specific fine-tuning — relevant because none of the off-the-shelf multilingual models above are fine-tuned for entity matching specifically. [Pre-trained Embeddings for Entity Resolution: An Experimental Analysis (arXiv:2304.12329)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12329)
- The blocking-vs-NN-search comparison paper found FAISS-based dense kNN blocking to be "by far the fastest and most scalable" approach as dataset size grows, while classical blocking workflows scale far worse in wall-clock time (the paper's synthetic-scaling numbers, from 10K→2M entities, show classical blocking workflows slowing by roughly 3-4 orders of magnitude more than FAISS/SCANN over that range) — though at smaller scale, classical blocking retained a precision/PQ edge. [arXiv:2202.12521](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12521)

### Realistic GPU throughput for encoding ~10-13M short records

Reference points, roughly ordered small→large model:

- `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` (English-only, 22M params): HuggingFace/sentence-transformers reports **~14,200 sentences/sec on a V100 GPU** vs. ~50 sentences/sec on a single CPU thread. [sbert.net pretrained models docs](https://www.sbert.net/docs/sentence_transformer/pretrained_models.html)
- `multi-qa-mpnet-base` class model (~110M params): sentence-transformers' own benchmark table reports **~4,000 queries/sec on GPU** vs. ~170/sec on CPU (unspecified GPU generation in the doc). [sbert.net pretrained models docs](https://www.sbert.net/docs/sentence_transformer/pretrained_models.html)
- Stock `sentence-transformers.encode()` with a multilingual-E5-class model (multilingual-e5-small/e5-small-v2) on a **single A100**: reported **~2,500 messages/sec** out of the box, rising to ~45,000 msg/s with basic custom batching, and to **~35,000–254,000 msg/s per GPU with a fully custom BF16 inference pipeline across 8×A100s** processing 685M short, noisy, multilingual social-media messages in 31.9 minutes wall-clock (a purpose-built high-throughput system, not representative of a quick hackathon setup). [Embedding 685 million texts in 32 minutes](https://dev.to/artain/embedding-685-million-texts-in-32-minutes-46o7)

**Back-of-envelope for this challenge** (~12.5M records total across S1+S2+S3, short business-name+address strings, single GPU, stock sentence-transformers, no custom kernel work): using the conservative **stock-pipeline anchor of ~2,500 records/sec on a single A100-class GPU**, encoding ~12.5M records ≈ 12.5M / 2,500 ≈ **5,000 seconds ≈ 1.4 hours**. Using a more pessimistic single-T4/A10G anchor around 500–1,000 records/sec (larger multilingual-e5-base/large model, smaller/older GPU, no fp16 tuning), the same job is roughly **3.5–7 hours**. Either figure fits inside a fraction of a 2-day (48-hour) hackathon budget, though the pessimistic end starts to compete meaningfully with iteration time if repeated for multiple experiments.

## (C) Hybrid: classical blocking + embedding cosine similarity as a GBT feature

This is architecturally a variant of (A) with an added feature column, not a separately benchmarked "family" in the literature — no toolkit or paper surveyed treats it as a first-class named approach, so the evidence here is assembled from adjacent primary sources rather than a dedicated hybrid-ER benchmark:

- The general pattern of "embed unstructured text, feed the embedding (or a similarity computed from it) into a GBT model like XGBoost alongside structured features" is a documented, common practice for combining semantic and tabular signal, sometimes called "hybrid boosted trees." [Combining XGBoost and Embeddings: Hybrid Semantic Boosted Trees?](https://machinelearningmastery.com/combining-xgboost-and-embeddings-hybrid-semantic-boosted-trees/)
- Within ER specifically, py_entitymatching / Magellan's feature-vector-then-classifier pipeline is designed to accept arbitrary similarity functions per attribute pair (edit distance, Jaccard, etc.), and an embedding-cosine-similarity column is a drop-in addition to that feature vector without changing the blocking stage — i.e., this hybrid design is a natural, low-risk extension of the existing Magellan-style pipeline rather than a new architecture. [py_entitymatching guides](http://magellan-example-test.org/packages/py-entitymatching/docs/user_manual/guides.html)
- CompanyName2Vec is one of the few domain-specific (company-name) studies that quantifies how much a semantic/embedding signal adds over pure string-similarity baselines for business names specifically: trained on ~10M job ads with a Bi-LSTM character-n-gram embedding of company names, it reports **Success@1 ≈ 76.9%** (probability the correct match is the top-ranked candidate) vs. **~67% for edit-distance (Levenshtein)** and **~72% for fuzzy matching** baselines, and separately **91% recall vs. 73% for baseline approaches** in a related report — a concrete illustration of the size of accuracy lift an embedding *signal* can add on top of string-similarity for exactly this challenge's domain (noisy business names), even though CompanyName2Vec itself is a standalone embedding matcher rather than a GBT-feature hybrid. Also explicitly noted: "robust to legal form, location, and business line variations, but synonym handling for rare/ambiguous abbreviations is limited." [CompanyName2Vec: Company Entity Matching Based on Job Ads (arXiv:2201.04687)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04687)
- Because hybrid (C) keeps classical blocking as the sole candidate-generation stage, it inherits family (A)'s blocking recall ceiling and the CorpFam candidate-generation-gap caveat above verbatim — the embedding feature can only re-rank/re-score pairs that the classical blocker already retrieved, it cannot recover matches the blocker drops. [Corporate-Family Resolution Is Not a String-Matching Problem (arXiv:2609.04269)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04269)

## FAISS / HNSW ANN feasibility at ~10M-vector scale

Primary-source numbers from the official FAISS wiki (not a third-party benchmark):

- **SIFT1M (1M × 128-d vectors), single Xeon E5-2680 v2 CPU node:** exhaustive Flat search takes 9.1s; `IVF16384,Flat` builds in **240s** and searches in **0.538s** at 0.898 recall@1; the same IVF index on a Titan X GPU builds in **5s** and searches in **0.059s** at 0.8145 recall@1. HNSW-Flat (single-threaded) achieves **0.020ms/query at 0.949 recall@1** (efSearch=32) up to **0.104ms/query at 0.992 recall@1** (efSearch=256). [FAISS wiki: Indexing 1M vectors](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/wiki/Indexing-1M-vectors)
- **Guidance for 10M-scale (10M–100M range):** FAISS's own index-selection guide recommends `IVF65536_HNSW32` — i.e. IVF clustering with HNSW used only for the coarse-quantizer cluster assignment step (not GPU-supported; use plain IVF for GPU). Training this index needs "between 30×65536 and 256×65536" (~2M–16.8M) training vectors. For 100M–1B scale, the guide steps up to `IVF262144_HNSW32` / `IVF1048576_HNSW32` and warns index training itself becomes slow. Pure HNSW (no IVF) is described as "the best option" when memory is not constrained, at a memory cost of `(d×4 + M×2×4)` bytes/vector. [FAISS wiki: Guidelines to choose an index](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/wiki/Guidelines-to-choose-an-index)
- **hnswlib** (the library FAISS's HNSW mode is based on) documents `M` (default 16) as the main memory/accuracy knob and `ef_construction` (default 200) as the main build-speed/search-speed tradeoff knob, but its own README does not publish concrete million-scale build-time/QPS numbers — those must be benchmarked directly for this project's hardware/data rather than assumed from the README. [nmslib/hnswlib GitHub](https://github.com/nmslib/hnswlib)
- Third-party but load-bearing sanity check at the actual scale of interest: a benchmark on a **10M-vector, 768-dim** dataset (matching this challenge's likely E5-base embedding size) reported HNSW query latency of **0.42ms p95** at **~11,200 QPS**, versus IVF at 0.83ms p95 with 3× less RAM and a build time ~2.3× faster than HNSW's at 10M scale (widening to ~4× at 100M scale) — i.e., HNSW query cost at 10M vectors is negligible relative to a 2-day budget, but its build time and RAM cost are the binding constraints to plan around, especially since AWS compute here is not pre-provisioned and must be spun up first. [FAISS IVF vs HNSW vs Flat: 10M Vector Query Latency Benchmarked](https://markaicode.com/benchmarks/faiss-production-benchmark-latency/)

## Recommendation

**Commit to (C), the hybrid: classical multi-key blocking (token + phonetic + postal/country) + a GBT classifier (XGBoost/LightGBM) whose feature set is extended with an embedding-cosine-similarity column (multilingual-e5-small or -base, MIT-licensed) computed only over records that already survive blocking.** Do not build (B)'s ANN-blocking + reranker pipeline from scratch for this hackathon; do not settle for (A) with string similarity alone.

**Why, against the four asked-for axes:**

- **Theoretical F_0.5 ceiling.** F_0.5 weights precision 2x over recall, so the ceiling is set less by "can we find more true matches" and more by "can we avoid false merges while still catching the matches blocking hands us." Pure classical features (Jaccard/Levenshtein/TF-IDF) already give a GBT strong precision on typo/abbreviation-level noise, but they are blind to the semantic-but-not-lexical cases this dataset explicitly contains — DBA/trade names, word-order transpositions, and Hindi-script transliteration in Source 2 — where two strings can be the same business with near-zero token overlap. CompanyName2Vec's controlled business-name comparison shows an embedding signal lifting Success@1 from ~67% (Levenshtein) / ~72% (fuzzy) to ~76.9%, and separately 91% vs. 73% recall over string-similarity baselines on exactly this kind of noisy company-name data (arXiv:2201.04687) — that gap is what hybrid buys, added as one more feature the GBT can learn to weight per-country rather than trust blindly. A from-scratch cross-encoder/Ditto-style matcher (96.5% F1 reported, arXiv:2004.00584) has a higher asymptotic ceiling but needs a fine-tuning budget and labeled-data volume this project does not have in two days; hybrid captures most of the accessible lift at a fraction of the engineering risk.
- **Blocking recall ceiling / reduction ratio at ~2.2M×(5M+5.3M) scale.** The controlled blocking-vs-NN-search study shows classical blocking workflows tuned to Pairs Completeness ≥ 0.9 at scales up to 2M entities (arXiv:2202.12521), and Splink's own benchmark dedupes 7M records with composite blocking rules in 2–25 minutes (robinlinacre.com/fast_deduplication) — so a well-built multi-key classical blocker (token blocking OR'd with phonetic keys OR'd with postal/country) is expected to clear a recall ceiling comfortably above what a rushed, first-time ANN index would achieve, without the added risk surface of FAISS/HNSW index tuning under a hard deadline. The one real risk classical blocking carries — "invisible" pairs with zero shared surface tokens, which one 2026 benchmark found 93.2% of never reach the candidate set at all (arXiv:2609.04269) — is a narrower failure mode than it sounds for this dataset: most of the noise patterns called out in README.md (abbreviation, typo, transliteration, word-order transposition) still share partial tokens or phonetic keys, unlike the arXiv:2609.04269 corporate-subsidiary case where names are genuinely unrelated strings. Hybrid inherits this same blocking stage as (A) — it does not fix the "invisible pairs" edge case — but that stage is the cheap, well-understood, already-in-progress part of the pipeline (the baseline ticket is building it in parallel), so hybrid reuses it rather than replacing it with untested infrastructure.
- **Wall-clock cost within the remaining 2-day budget.** Classical blocking is minutes at multi-million scale (Splink: 7M records, ~2–25 min). GBT training on engineered features is CPU-only and fast. The only new cost hybrid adds is encoding business-name text with a small multilingual embedding model — back-of-envelope, even encoding the full ~12.5M-record corpus is ~1.4h on a single A100 (stock sentence-transformers pipeline, ~2,500 rec/s) to ~3.5–7h on a smaller T4/A10G (docs/research figures above), and in practice hybrid only needs to embed records that survive blocking, a far smaller set. Full (B) requires all of that encoding cost *plus* building and tuning a FAISS/HNSW index at 10M-vector scale (FAISS's own guidance flags index build time and RAM, not query latency, as the binding constraint at this scale) *plus* training and validating a cross-encoder or second reranking stage — three new pieces of infrastructure to build, debug, and calibrate from a cold start, none of which has a published evaluation on short noisy business-name text. That is a lot of unproven engineering risk to take on inside a 48-hour window that also has to leave room for the traceability report (issue #7), country-specific error analysis, and threshold calibration. Hybrid's only new component is one feature column, which is easy to ablate, debug, and fall back from if it underperforms.
- **License / param-count compliance.** multilingual-e5-small (MIT, 118M params) or multilingual-e5-base (MIT, 278M params) both clear the MIT/Apache-2.0 and ≤8B-parameter requirements with enormous headroom, as do XGBoost (Apache-2.0) and LightGBM (MIT). No fair-play risk: embedding a business name with a pretrained open model is not an external data lookup.

**For whoever implements issue #11:** build the classical multi-key blocker (token blocking, phonetic keys, postal/country blocking, OR'd together) exactly as family (A) would need it, run every surviving candidate pair's business-name text through multilingual-e5-small or -base to get a single cosine-similarity feature, add it to the existing hand-engineered similarity feature set (Jaccard, Levenshtein, TF-IDF cosine, address token overlap), and train one GBT (XGBoost or LightGBM) classifier on the combined feature vector. Do not add an ANN blocking stage or a cross-encoder reranker — that is explicitly out of scope for this recommendation given the time budget. If blocking recall on the held-out split (per the issue #2 validation strategy) looks low specifically on cases with near-zero token overlap, the cheapest mitigation is adding another classical blocking key (e.g., a looser phonetic key or n-gram blocking), not switching to ANN blocking mid-hackathon.
